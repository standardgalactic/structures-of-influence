**Central Thesis**

The central thesis of this lecture is that nominal price rigidity—where firms adjust prices infrequently or only in response to long-term strategic considerations—does not invalidate standard price theory. The speaker argues that such behavior demonstrates the persistence of fundamental supply and demand dynamics, thereby maintaining the validity of classical economic principles even when faced with short-term fluctuations.

**Argument Reconstruction**

The argument unfolds through several key points:

1. **Empirical Evidence from Real-World Data**: The speaker emphasizes empirical evidence from surveys (e.g., Lord Keynes & Paul Walter's 2000 report) showing that a majority of UK manufacturing firms adjust their prices at specific frequencies, with many changing only once annually or quarterly. This indicates that price adjustments are not always immediate responses to demand changes but can be scheduled based on strategic considerations.

2. **Dominance of Cost-Based Pricing**: Survey data reveal that 53.8% of firms rely on constant marginal costs, and 47.1% use implicit or explicit contracts. These findings suggest that cost considerations are more influential than market signaling through price changes alone.

3. **Illustrative Examples from Industry Practices**: The speaker uses concrete examples such as Tesco’s handling of beef inventory to demonstrate how supermarkets can maintain surplus stock without immediate price reductions, thereby preserving revenue streams and leveraging market leverage over suppliers like Hilton Food Group.

4. **Contrast with Post-Keynesian Interpretations**: By contrasting these empirical observations with post-Keynesian critiques that argue nominal price rigidity undermines standard theory, the speaker positions his interpretation as a rebuttal to those who view such short-term data as indicative of deeper economic flaws.
