**Central Thesis**

The lecture presents an argument that ideological identities—classical liberalism, conservatism/fascism, and socialism—are fundamentally antagonistic to one another due to their distinct core values, which leads to a recurring pattern of labeling opponents as extremist or totalitarian (the “horseshoe” phenomenon). This thesis is grounded in the moral foundations theory proposed by Jonathan Haidt, which categorizes moral concerns into negative liberty, fairness, authority, loyalty, and sanctity. Each ideological camp perceives these values as threats to its own foundational principles, resulting in perpetual accusations of extremism.

**Argument Reconstruction**

The speaker argues that classical liberalism prioritizes individual liberty, viewing both conservatism/fascism and socialism as attempts to control individuals and dictate behavior. Conservatives/fascists emphasize the preservation of homelands, cultures, and people, seeing liberalism and socialism as direct threats to national identity and order. Socialists advocate for equality of outcomes and see any opposition—especially from liberals and conservatives—as undermining their egalitarian goals.

This antagonism is not merely ideological but stems from a perceived threat to core values: liberty for liberals; authority, loyalty, and sanctity for conservatives/fascists; and equality of outcomes for socialists. Consequently, each side tends to label opponents as totalitarian or extremist, creating an “infinite horseshoe” pattern where all three ideologies are seen as occupying the same extreme ends of a spectrum.

The argument is anchored in Jonathan Haidt’s Moral Foundations Theory, which categorizes moral concerns into five distinct foundations: negative liberty (liberty for liberals), fairness (shared concern across groups but emphasized differently), authority (authority, loyalty, sanctity for conservatives/fascists), loyalty (loyalty to group and nation), and sanctity (sanctity of tradition and purity). These concepts provide a framework for understanding how each ideology perceives threats from the others.

Historical examples are drawn from classical liberal thinkers like John Locke and Adam Smith, who emphasized individual rights and limited government; conservative/fascist figures such as Edmund Burke and Adolf Hitler/Benito Mussolini, who stressed national identity and authoritarian governance; and socialist theorists like Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin, whose focus on class struggle and equality of outcomes contrasts sharply with the other two ideologies.
