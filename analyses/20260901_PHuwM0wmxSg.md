**Central Thesis**

José Ortega y Gasset's central thesis in *The Revolt of the Masses* is that modern liberal societies have succumbed to a pervasive “mass man” mentality, characterized by conformity, radical ingratitude toward civilization’s benefits, and an arrogant dismissal of historical wisdom. This mass man—representing the common populace—is defined not by social class but by character traits such as lack of self‑esteem, conformist behavior, and a belief that they deserve entitlements without corresponding obligations or self‑restraint. Ortega argues that this mindset represents a cultural pathology where individualism is proclaimed yet systematically replaced by conformity through the reduction of all values to the lowest common denominator (quantity over quality), threatening the stability and vitality of complex societies.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Ortega y Gasset delineates three interrelated arguments:

1. **Characterization of the Mass Man**: The “mass man” is not defined by economic status but by a set of psychological traits—conformism, radical ingratitude toward civilization’s benefits, and an inherent belief in entitlement without merit or effort. This individual embodies mediocrity as a virtue, viewing inherited privileges (security, rights, technology) as natural rather than earned.

2. **Cultural Pathology**: The prevailing cultural ethos of modern liberal societies reduces all values to mere quantity, eroding the pursuit of excellence and deference to higher purpose. Ortega posits that this reflects an underlying pathology where societal structures systematically suppress individuality in favor of uniformity, leading to a regression toward base instincts akin to evolutionary theory’s “natural end point.”

3. **Revolutionary Implications**: The dominance of the mass man in cultural and political spheres threatens civilizational stability by replacing disciplined, deferential hierarchies with unqualified majoritarianism. This shift undermines the qualities—discipline, deference to excellence, acceptance of hierarchy—that have historically sustained complex societies.
