**Central Thesis**

Erich Fromm argues that modern society creates alienation through the prioritization of instrumental rationality—efficiency and productivity over human fulfillment—and consequently traps individuals in a “society of mass” where authentic self‑expression is devalued, leading to isolation and meaninglessness. This thesis is supported by historical examples such as Thomas Carlyle’s influence on social psychology, Isaiah Berlin’s distinction between negative and positive liberty, and Jean-Paul Sartre’s existentialist critique of freedom avoidance.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Fromm contends that the shift toward instrumental rationality—where human values are subordinated to efficiency—produces a “society of mass” characterized by alienation. Individuals become detached from authentic relationships, rootedness, meaning, and productive work, resulting in a sense of dread rather than genuine liberation. The critique extends beyond personal psychology to institutional structures (e.g., capitalism, consumer culture) that fail to provide positive freedom, leading to psychological discomfort.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

Key intellectual sources include Thomas Carlyle’s influence on social psychology themes, Isaiah Berlin’s concepts of negative versus positive liberty, and Jean-Paul Sartre’s existentialist philosophy emphasizing the avoidance of total freedom. Fromm builds upon these ideas by applying them to contemporary consumerism and mass media.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

Fromm illustrates his argument with historical examples such as Carlyle’s observations on social psychology, Berlin’s critique of totalitarian control over personal liberty, and Sartre’s existential dread when confronting freedom without responsibility. Contemporary illustrations include the COVID‑19 pandemic, which re‑established old forms of conformity and authority, reinforcing the idea that modern liberal frameworks may not sustain positive freedom.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

The argument assumes a pervasive cultural shift toward instrumental rationality that marginalizes humanistic values, structural embedding of alienation in social institutions (e.g., capitalism), and historical continuity between past forms of oppression (like religious dogma) and contemporary consumerist ideologies. Unresolved questions include whether solutions like fostering authentic relationships are empirically effective without creating new dependencies or control.

**Relation to the Study of Institutional and Cultural Influence**

Fromm’s analysis directly addresses how institutional structures—such as mass media, advertising, and bureaucratic workplaces—shape cultural norms that prioritize efficiency over human fulfillment. This aligns with broader studies in sociology and political science on how institutions influence societal values, identity formation, and individual well‑being.

**SECTION 6**

**Claims and Reasoning**

1. **Hyperreality and Loneliness**: Modern mass media and industrial/office models create a “hyperreality” where individuals experience belonging without genuine interpersonal connection, leading to loneliness.

2. **Passivity Through Division**: When planning (designing) and execution (carrying out tasks) are separated—common in factories or offices—workers become passive, performing duties they did not design and unable to see the larger context of their work. This fosters passivity and disconnection from the product.

3. **Need for Community Roots**: People require a sense of community rooted in place and culture; otherwise, they experience rootlessness and belonging nowhere. Fromm advocates for cooperative and communitarian forms of work where individuals can trace their contributions to the finished product, thereby restoring the link between self and action.

**Named Thinkers, Works, Concepts, and Historical Examples**

- **Erich Fromm**: Social psychologist and psychoanalyst; works include *The Art of Loving* (1956) and *Escape from Freedom* (1941).
  
- **McDonaldization**: Concept by George Ritzer (1993), describing the rationalization of cultural institutions—similar to how Fromm critiques industrial/office structures as creating a hyperreality.

**Assumptions Required**

1. Modern mass media and consumer culture contribute significantly to feelings of alienation.
2. Historical examples such as early 20th‑century factory work illustrate similar dynamics of disconnection from labor outcomes.
3. Genuine community engagement can alleviate loneliness, currently lacking in many societies.

- **Unresolved Questions**:
