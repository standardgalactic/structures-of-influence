**Central Thesis**

The lecture argues that since around 1999, popular culture has entered a phase of pronounced stagnation characterized by superficiality, derivative aesthetics, and the dominance of market forces over authentic artistic expression. This cultural decline is interpreted as an outgrowth of late capitalism’s economic logic, which erodes depth, meaning, and genuine emotional engagement in favor of surface‑driven style and commodified “pastiche.” The analysis draws heavily on Fredric Jameson’s framework for understanding postmodernism as the cultural manifestation of late capitalist dynamics, contrasting it with earlier modernist narratives that emphasized critical distance from dominant ideologies.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Fredric Jameson posits that postmodern culture reflects the structural realities of late capitalism—globalization, financial dominance, and information‑driven economies. Under these conditions, cultural forms exhibit a pronounced loss of depth: traditional emotional resonance is supplanted by free‑floating euphoria or anxiety detached from any clear cause (Jameson, *Postmodernism, or The Cultural Logic of Late Capitalism*). This shift manifests in the proliferation of “pastiche,” where new iterations build upon past styles without adding substantive innovation. Examples such as Beethoven’s deep emotional resonance contrasted with pop music like the Beatles’ “She Loves Me” illustrate how authenticity and originality have waned.

Jameson distinguishes this cultural stagnation from Jean‑François Lyotard’s view of postmodernism as an artistic liberation liberated from grand narratives. Instead, he argues that postmodernism is a symptom of late capitalism’s economic imperatives, where consumer behavior—referred to as “slot pigs”—actively desires products aligned with consumption habits, reinforcing the idea that cultural hierarchies between high and low art have largely dissolved into market‑driven distinctions (Jameson). This analysis underscores how artistic output increasingly serves to naturalize existing capitalist relations rather than challenge them.
