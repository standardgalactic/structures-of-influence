**Central Thesis**

The lecture argues that Haiti’s persistent economic decline from the late 18th century to the present is fundamentally rooted in a series of coercive and structurally regressive policies—both internal (such as inheritance laws, forced labor systems, and authoritarian regimes) and external (U.S. military occupation and intervention). These policies perpetuated dependency on plantation agriculture, limited economic diversification, and entrenched elite control over political power, thereby preventing Haiti from achieving sustainable development.

**Argument Reconstruction**

The speaker’s argument unfolds in several interrelated stages:

1. **Post‑Revolutionary Economic Shock:** Following the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), the population fell dramatically due to war casualties and expulsions of French colonists, signaling severe economic disruption. The collapse of sugar plantations—Haiti’s primary export—reduced production levels from pre‑revolutionary highs to a fraction of those levels, indicating a fundamental shift in Haiti’s economic base.

2. **Structural Economic Policies:** The emergence of a mulatto elite and elements of the former black Haitian military sought to preserve the colonial plantation system by maintaining control over labor without allowing free peasants to challenge their authority. This desire for preservation manifested in policies that perpetuated forced labor through inheritance laws, ensuring continual subdivision of land holdings among heirs.

3. **Continuation of Forced Labor:** The speaker emphasizes that economic stagnation persisted from 1809 onward due to the structural effects of these inheritance rules, which reduced productive capacity per household and maintained a “permanent peasantry” reliant on subsistence agriculture. This perpetuated low GDP per capita despite population growth.

4. **Soil Erosion and Agricultural Decline:** Because peasants relied exclusively on their own holdings without implementing crop rotation or communal labor practices, soil erosion became severe, further limiting agricultural productivity and economic resilience.

5. **Political Instability and Elite Dominance:** Land ownership tied to inheritance led to high office becoming the exclusive preserve of a ruling elite, fostering factionalism and coups (1843–1915). This political volatility exacerbated social turmoil and hindered long‑term development efforts.

6. **External Intervention and Dependency:** The speaker argues that U.S. military occupation from 1915 to 1934 temporarily halted intra‑state conflict, allowing for economic development projects but also establishing a dependency structure where external powers controlled Haiti’s political trajectory through authoritarian regimes (Duvalier and Baby Doc). These regimes consolidated power through terror, vodou clergy, and local enforcers, further entrenching elite dominance.

7. **Legacy of Authoritarian Rule:** The rise of François Duvalier (“Papa Doc”) and his successor Jean‑Claude (“Baby Doc”) exemplified how authoritarian rule maintained elite control over the state, leading to massive wealth accumulation by elites (e.g., Baby Doc amassed $1.6 billion by 1986). Post‑1986 turmoil, culminating in the 2010 earthquake, left Haiti among the poorest nations globally.
