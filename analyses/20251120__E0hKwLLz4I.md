**Central Thesis**

Anthony Sutton argued that during the Cold War, Wall Street (the capitalist banking class) pursued a “captive market” strategy in the Soviet Union to preserve monopolistic control over its industries—from raw material extraction through advanced manufacturing. According to Sutton’s three‑volume work *Western Technology and Soviet Economic Development*, much of the post‑World II industrial boom in the USSR was driven by foreign capitalist corporations that invested heavily in Soviet factories and infrastructure rather than solely through internal socialist planning.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Sutton contended that Stalin’s expropriation policies were a reaction to these external investments, suggesting that rapid Soviet industry expansion could have been sustained even without state‑directed collectivist efforts if left to capitalist interests. The core thesis posits that the USSR was economically dependent on Western capital for technological advancement, which justified continued economic pressure from Western powers and framed the Cold War in terms of market control rather than ideological confrontation alone.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

- **Anthony Sutton (1925–2002)**: Research fellow at the Hoover Institution; author of *Western Technology and Soviet Economic Development* (three volumes covering 1917‑1930, 1930‑1945, and 1945‑1965).
- **Bjinski’s “Two Ages”**: Incorporates Sutton’s research to argue that economic pressure on the USSR was justified because Soviets were dependent on Western capital for technological advancement.
- **Richard Pipes’ *Survival Is Not Enough***: Cites Sutton’s thesis as part of his argument that the West should maintain economic leverage over the Soviet Union, arguing that innovation without external assistance would be impossible.

**Assumptions Required**

1. The existence of a monopolistic market interest among Wall Street and other capitalist corporations.
2. Historical context of Cold War reluctance to disclose full details due to political motivations (Stalin’s anti‑capitalist rhetoric and Reagan administration’s ideological stance).
3. Sufficiency of foreign investment alone for sustaining rapid Soviet industrialization without extensive socialist planning or state intervention.

**Qualifications, Objections, and Unresolved Questions**

- **Academic Reception vs. Popular Perception**: While Sutton’s scholarly works were well received academically, his more popular writings have been heavily scrutinized by historians and economists who question the validity of his claims about Soviet industrialization.
- **Counterarguments from Historians**:
  - Some scholars argue that rapid Soviet industry growth was primarily driven by internal socialist policies (e.g., Five-Year Plans) rather than foreign investment.
  - Others contend that Sutton’s analysis overlooks bureaucratic inefficiencies and misallocation within the Soviet system, which hindered innovation.

- **Unresolved Questions**
  - To what extent did foreign capital influence Soviet technological advancements compared to domestic planning?
  - How accurately do Sutton’s claims reflect actual economic dynamics of the period without ideological bias?
