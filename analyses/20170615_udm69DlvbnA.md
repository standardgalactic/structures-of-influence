**Central Thesis**

The lecture argues that realpolitik—originally articulated by Niccolò Machiavelli in *The Prince* (cited before the 1520s)—advocates prioritizing practical power interests over ideological or moral considerations when dealing with rulers, especially those occupying newly conquered territories. Machiavelli’s central claim is that a ruler should be feared rather than loved because loyalty and love are unreliable under pressure, whereas fear ensures obedience through the threat of punishment.

**Argument Reconstruction**

1. **Realpolitik as a Pragmatic Approach**: The speaker contends that realpolitik extends beyond individual rulers to nation‑states by drawing analogies with 19th‑century geopolitics (the “great game” between Britain and Russia over Afghanistan) and later the Cold War rivalry between NATO and the Warsaw Pact blocs. This extension suggests that foreign policy should be guided by strategic interests—maintaining a buffer, securing trade routes, or containing rival power blocks—rather than ideological alignment.

2. **Application to Nation‑States**: The principle is applied to nation‑states through historical examples such as:
   - **The “great game”** between Britain and Russia over Afghanistan (19th century), illustrating Machiavellian pragmatism in international relations.
   - **Cold War rivalry** between NATO (United States and allies) and the Warsaw Pact blocs, showing how nation‑states used realpolitik to align with or oppose regimes based on strategic interests rather than ideological compatibility.
