**Central Thesis**

Liberalism fails to maintain enduring social order because it privileges abstract individual rights and limited government at the expense of traditional hierarchies, sacred authority, and communal values that historically have been necessary to sustain stability.

**Argument Reconstruction**

1. **Authority Precedes Consent**: De Maistre argues that legitimate authority must precede popular consent; otherwise, order cannot be rationally grounded (De Maistre, 1785). This premise is foundational because it posits that without a pre‑existing source of legitimacy, any political system relying solely on democratic consent will inevitably falter.

2. **Sacred Majesty and Tradition**: Monarchies derive their force from a sacred aura and historical continuity (Chesterton’s fence), whereas modern liberal systems reduce authority to utility or popular approval, leading to loss of inherent majesty and making obedience feel coerced rather than natural (De Maistre, 1785).

3. **Limits of Written Constitutions**: De Maistre contends that constitutions engineered without deep roots in custom, religion, and historical loyalty remain perpetually fragile because legalism alone cannot sustain durable social structures (De Maistre, 1785). This suggests that formal documents must be anchored in lived tradition to function effectively.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

- **Thomas Hobbes**: Leviathan (1651) posits a social contract where authority precedes consent to maintain order.
- **Robert Filmer**: Patriarcha (1687) argues for the divine right of kings based on biblical precedent.
- **Carl Schmitt**: The concept of “enemies” and the sovereign’s ability to define enemies is echoed in De Maistre’s discussion of authority versus consent.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

- **French Revolution (1789‑1799)**: Used as an example of what happens when legitimacy is reduced to utility or popular vote, leading to chaos and loss of majesty.
- **Ancient Roman Republic**: Illustrated how liberal democratic principles can lead to instability when unchecked by strong central authority.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

- The assumption that societies are prone to periods of disorder (riots, protests) where established norms break down.
- The belief that rational or humanitarian frameworks alone cannot adequately address such crises without resorting to coercive measures.
- Historical continuity is assumed across different contexts, suggesting a timeless aspect to social order maintenance.

**Qualifications and Counterarguments**

- **Objection from Liberal Scholars**: Critics argue that liberal democracies have historically managed crises without extreme authoritarian measures (e.g., post‑World War II period in Western Europe), challenging the notion of inherent failure.
- **Question of Alternatives**: Whether alternative forms of governance or social mechanisms could replace coercive force, such as non‑violent collective action, community policing, or technological solutions.

**Relation to the Study of Institutional and Cultural Influence**

The argument highlights how institutional structures (e.g., constitutions) and cultural values (e.g., tradition, sacred authority) shape political stability. It suggests that liberalism’s emphasis on abstract rationalism may conflict with societies’ inherent need for traditional hierarchies and moral frameworks, influencing long‑term social cohesion.
