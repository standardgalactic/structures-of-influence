**Central Thesis**

John Gray argues that liberal democratic ideals—particularly those associated with individual liberty, equality before the law, and market‑driven economies—are fundamentally flawed when applied to real‑world politics. His thesis is rooted in historical evidence showing how “utopian” systems such as neoliberalism have failed to produce durable peace or understanding; instead, they often exacerbate social divisions by working against the natural tendencies of human nature.

**Argument Reconstruction**

1. **Human Nature and Conflict**: Gray begins by asserting that humans are fundamentally animalistic beings subject to evolutionary pressures, leading to perpetual conflict, war, and violence regardless of social conditioning (cf. Hobbes’ *Leviathan*). This premise sets a foundational expectation that liberal ideals must contend with inherent human tendencies toward self‑interest.

2. **Liberal Ideals as Illusory**: He contends that the liberal ideal—often linked to individual autonomy, rational choice, and free markets—is an illusion when translated into practice. Historical examples such as the Jacobin Revolution (1790s), Bolshevik Russia (1917), and contemporary humanitarian interventions illustrate how liberal rhetoric has been co‑opted for authoritarian ends.

3. **Illusion of Self and Free Will**: Gray critiques modern liberalism’s assumption that individuals possess a free will capable of rational decision‑making, drawing on Western philosophical traditions like utilitarianism (John Stuart Mill) and Chicago School public choice theory. He argues these assumptions are unrealistic because human behavior is driven by unconscious processes—evolutionary inheritance, instinct, cultural conditioning—that operate beneath conscious awareness.

4. **Technology as a Double‑Edged Sword**: Gray emphasizes that technological progress does not inherently improve morality or reduce violence; rather, it amplifies existing impulses for destruction and conflict (e.g., the escalation of weaponry during World War I and II). This underscores his view that technology’s impact is contingent on human intent.

5. **Liberalism as a Utopian Project**: He asserts that classical liberal theory—promoting free markets and minimal state intervention—is itself an artificial order, requiring active enforcement rather than being a natural outcome (referencing F. A. Hayek). This challenges the notion of liberalism as a spontaneous social arrangement.

6. **Value Pluralism**: Gray argues for value pluralism, asserting that there is no single best way of living applicable to all humanity. He draws on Arthur de Gobineau’s “civilization is incompatible” thesis and Joseph Schumpeter’s idea that nations develop unique forms of socialism suited to their cultural contexts.
