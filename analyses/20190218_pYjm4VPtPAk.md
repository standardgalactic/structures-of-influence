**Central Thesis**

The central thesis of the lecture is that the “tendency towards equilibrium” in market prices reflects a fundamental principle by which markets naturally balance supply and demand under normal conditions. This equilibrium concept posits that when the quantity supplied matches the quantity demanded, price adjustments ensure that no participant in the market can profitably exploit an imbalance.

**Argument Reconstruction**

The speaker reconstructs this argument through illustrative examples of how changes in demand affect biscuit prices:

1. **Increased Demand for Biscuits**: When there is greater consumer desire for biscuits than what producers currently supply, the price rises. This price increase serves two functions: it incentivizes existing biscuit makers to produce more (higher profit per unit) and signals potential entrepreneurs that expanding production could be profitable.

2. **Decreased Demand for Biscuits**: Conversely, if demand shifts toward another product like cakes, the price of biscuits falls because producers now compete for fewer customers. The falling price acts as a signal to some biscuit producers that their current output is not profitable and should be reduced or discontinued.

These examples demonstrate how market prices adjust according to supply-demand dynamics, embodying the “tendency towards equilibrium” insofar as competition remains free within the market.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

Although the lecturer does not explicitly cite historical economists by name, the principles discussed align with classical economic thought:

- **Adam Smith**: In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Smith introduced the concept of the “invisible hand,” suggesting that market forces naturally lead to equilibrium without central planning.
  
- **David Ricardo**: His work on comparative advantage and price adjustments in *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817) supports the idea that markets will self-correct through price signals.

- **Léon Walras**: Walras formalized general equilibrium theory in *Éléments d'économie politique pure* (1874), providing a mathematical framework for understanding how prices adjust to achieve market balance across all goods and services.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

The lecture relies on intuitive examples—such as the biscuit scenario—to illustrate how price adjustments reflect changes in supply and demand. These illustrations are meant to be accessible and relatable, demonstrating that equilibrium is not a static state but an ongoing process of adjustment driven by market participants' responses to price signals.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

Several implicit premises underpin the argument:

- **Competitive Market Assumption**: The analysis assumes markets are competitive with numerous buyers and sellers who have no power to influence prices independently.
  
- **Rational Behavior**: Producers are assumed to act rationally, aiming to maximize profit based on price signals without considering external factors like production costs or market entry barriers.

- **Absence of Distortions**: The argument implicitly assumes that there are no significant externalities (e.g., pollution) or government interventions (e.g., subsidies) that could prevent the market from reaching true equilibrium.

**Qualifications and Counterarguments**

Critics may argue that real-world markets often experience disequilibrium due to:

- **Market Power**: Monopolies or oligopolies can manipulate prices, preventing them from reflecting true supply-demand balances.
  
- **Information Asymmetries**: Unequal information distribution among market participants can lead to persistent price discrepancies.

- **External Shocks**: Events such as natural disasters or pandemics can disrupt equilibrium by altering either demand or supply unexpectedly.

**Relation to the Study of Institutional and Cultural Influence**

The concept of “tendency towards equilibrium” is deeply intertwined with institutional and cultural influences on economic behavior:

- **Institutional Factors**: Regulations, legal frameworks, and property rights institutions shape how markets operate. For instance, labor laws can affect production capacities (supply), while consumer protection regulations might influence demand patterns.

- **Cultural Influences**: Cultural attitudes toward risk, saving, and consumption preferences impact market dynamics. Societal norms about quality expectations or brand loyalty can alter perceived demand for certain goods.

Understanding these influences helps contextualize why equilibrium may not always be achieved in practice, as cultural and institutional factors introduce variability into the assumptions underlying classical economic theory.
