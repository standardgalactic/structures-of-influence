**Central Thesis**

The lecture by Neema Parvini argues that nationalism—particularly as it manifests in small nations—is inherently utopian, unrealistic, and often manipulated for geopolitical purposes by larger imperial powers. This thesis is built on a historical analysis of how nationalist sentiments have been co-opted to serve the strategic interests of dominant empires such as the United States or the former Soviet Union.

**Argument Reconstruction**

1. **Nationalism’s Utopian Nature**: Parvini contends that nationalism, in its abstract form, is utopian and unrealistic because it presumes a singular national identity can unify diverse populations without underlying socioeconomic disparities. This claim is supported by observations of various self‑avowed nationalist movements (e.g., white nationalists) which she perceives as being exploited for political gain.

2. **Differentiation Between Nationalisms**: She distinguishes between “narrow nationalism” prevalent in small nations like Belgium, Poland, and Taiwan—where national identity is closely tied to ethnic or linguistic cohesion—and what she terms “empire nationalism,” applicable only to large nation‑states with significant global influence (e.g., the United States or the former Soviet Union). Empire nationalism, for Parvini, serves broader geopolitical strategies rather than purely domestic solidarity.

3. **Historical Context of Imperialism**: Drawing on examples from the Cold War era, Parvini illustrates how the USA and USSR funded nationalist uprisings against existing European imperial powers (e.g., in Eastern Europe, Vietnam). This funding was part of a larger strategy to counterbalance each other’s influence, underscoring nationalism as a tool for geopolitical maneuvering.

4. **Current Geopolitical Dynamics**: By referencing contemporary events such as the Ukraine conflict, Parvini suggests that even “nationalist” movements like those in Ukraine are often proxies for American and Russian strategic interests, implying that national identities serve external agendas rather than purely internal solidarity.
