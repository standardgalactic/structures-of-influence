**Central Thesis**

The central thesis of the lecture is that traditional economic principles—specifically the law of supply and demand—are robustly supported by empirical evidence from historical case studies such as Coca‑Cola, thereby challenging post-Keynesian critiques which argue for widespread price rigidity in certain sectors. The argument posits that when firms like Coca‑Cola maintain fixed nominal prices despite significant changes in real purchasing power (due to inflation or deflation), this behavior aligns with standard economic theory rather than indicating fundamental market failures.

1. **Establishing the Principle:** Prices rise when demand exceeds supply and fall when supply exceeds demand—this is presented as an undisputed economic law.
   
2. **Contrasting Post‑Keynesian Views:** The speaker critiques post-Keynesians who argue that many firms do not adjust their prices frequently (e.g., 35% change annually, 5% never change). They cite nominal price rigidity in sectors like Coca‑Cola as evidence against the law of supply and demand.

3. **Illustrative Examples:** Using Coca‑Cola’s fixed pricing from 1886 to early 1950s, the speaker demonstrates that fluctuations in real prices (due to inflation or deflation) should have directly affected sales volume if price rigidity were not a factor. This is supported by historical sales data showing a dramatic increase in total demand during this period and subsequent drops during economic downturns.

4. **Interpreting Historical Data:** The speaker argues that the fixed pricing of Coca‑Cola reflects adherence to standard price theory, whereas post-Keynesians might attribute such stability to strategic branding or other non-economic factors rather than market necessity.
