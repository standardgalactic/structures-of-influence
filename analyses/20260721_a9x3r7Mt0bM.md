**Central Thesis**

Michael Oakeshott argues that contemporary politics is dominated by a belief that all political problems can be solved through abstract principles, technical rules, and ideological blueprints—a rationalist approach he terms “rationalism.” He contends that this reliance on abstract reasoning neglects the importance of practical knowledge gained from lived experience, tradition, and habit. Oakeshott distinguishes between *technical knowledge* (learned through manuals and formal education) and *practical knowledge* (developed through trial and error). According to him, technical knowledge alone is insufficient for addressing complex political issues because it overlooks the insights derived from practical experience.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Oakeshott identifies a fundamental limitation in modern political discourse: its pervasive belief that abstract principles can universally solve social problems. This rationalist mindset pervades various ideological spectrums—liberalism (e.g., John Stuart Mill), socialism (e.g., Karl Marx), and certain strands of conservatism and libertarianism that advocate for axiomatic first principles akin to Ludwig von Mises.

He critiques the notion that abstract reasoning can fully capture the complexities inherent in human social relations. Oakeshott argues that such a belief leads to an overreliance on formal education and intellectual discourse, which he sees as inadequate for navigating real-world political challenges. He emphasizes that practical knowledge—gained through habit, tradition, and lived experience—is richer and more applicable than theoretical constructs.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

- **John Stuart Mill**: A liberal thinker who advocated utilitarianism and the belief in rational discourse’s ability to solve social problems.
- **Karl Marx**: A socialist theorist whose works emphasize class struggle and economic determinants as primary drivers of societal change.
- **Ludwig von Mises**: An Austrian economist known for advocating free-market principles and individual choice.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

Oakeshott uses historical examples to illustrate his critique. For instance, he notes how liberal reforms (e.g., crime policy) have sometimes produced disastrous outcomes due to untested theories—illustrating the dangers of applying abstract principles without empirical validation.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

1. That abstract principles are universally applicable solutions to political problems.
2. That formal education and intellectual discourse can fully capture social complexities.
3. That there exists a universal method (rationalism) that remains valid across different cultures and historical contexts without loss of meaning or applicability.

**Qualifications, Objections, and Unresolved Questions**

- **Objection**: Critics might argue that rationalist approaches have historically led to significant societal improvements (e.g., advancements in medicine and technology), suggesting the problem lies not with rationalism itself but its misapplication.
- **Unresolved Question**: How can one measure the effectiveness of practical knowledge versus technical knowledge in specific political contexts? Is there a way to integrate both without leading to relativism or nihilism?
