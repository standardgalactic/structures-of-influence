**Central Thesis**

The central thesis of the lecture is that rent control policies—such as those implemented in New York City, Toronto, Melbourne, and other cities—are fundamentally ineffective at achieving their intended goals of stabilizing housing costs and increasing affordability. The argument posits that these policies create demand distortions, shift rationing mechanisms from price to chance or favoritism, reduce the supply of rental units, lead to severe housing shortages, and encourage suboptimal occupancy patterns.

**Argument Reconstruction**

The speaker reconstructs the argument by outlining several key points:

1. **Demand Distortions:** Rent control artificially lowers rent prices, making living spaces appear more affordable to a broader segment of the population. This increases demand for rental units beyond what would be available in a market without such controls.

2. **Shift in Rationing Mechanism:** According to Milton Friedman and George Stigler (1947), rent control changes housing allocation from a price-based rationing system to one based on chance or favoritism (e.g., personal connections with the mayor). This leads to inefficiencies in how units are distributed.

3. **Reduced Supply:** Rent controls discourage new construction by making rental properties less financially viable for developers, leading to a decrease in housing supply. Historical examples include:
   - Toronto’s 1975 rent control causing a 40% drop in building values over five years.
   - Santa Monica, California (1979), where building permits fell below 10% of previous levels.
   - England and Wales, where privately built rental housing dropped from 61% to just 14% between 1947 and 1977.

4. **Resulting Housing Shortages:** The reduction in supply combined with increased demand results in severe shortages, as seen in Melbourne (post‑World War Two) and San Francisco (2001), where controlled housing became more than half a century old on average.

5. **Occupancy Patterns:** Rent control leads tenants to occupy larger spaces than necessary for longer periods, exacerbating the shortage of smaller units for those who need them most. In New York City, 49% of controlled apartments house only one occupant despite significant housing shortages among immigrants and lower‑income residents.
