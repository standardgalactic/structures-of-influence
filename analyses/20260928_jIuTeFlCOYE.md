**Central Thesis**

The central thesis of Hillaire Belloc’s critique in *Why You Don’t Own Anything (and Think You’re Happy)* is that modern economic structures—particularly those enabled by the welfare state and concentrated capital—have eroded true ownership, leading to a condition he terms “servile” where individuals are legally compelled to work for elite interests while maintaining an illusion of autonomy. Belloc argues that this creates psychological alienation and undermines genuine happiness through dependency on external authorities rather than self-sustaining property rights.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Belloc’s argument unfolds in three interrelated parts:

1. **The Modern State as a Servile State:** Society becomes “servile” when legal structures (e.g., labor codes, welfare systems) compel the majority to work for elite interests. These mechanisms ensure mass dependency while preserving autonomy for elites.

2. **Concentrated Capital’s Threat:** Even if welfare systems appear benevolent, they maintain power dynamics by keeping masses dependent and elites autonomous through monopolistic control over production and distribution.

3. **Atomization and Alienation:** Modern mass media and hyperreality exacerbate feelings of atomization (isolation) and alienation (disconnection from communal values), intensifying the effects of a servile state.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

Belloc uses historical examples such as the Black Death’s impact on labor dynamics and medieval England’s customary tenures to illustrate how property rights historically provided economic security. He contrasts this with modern welfare systems that, while appearing to alleviate poverty, perpetuate dependency through fixed income structures tied to employment rather than productive capacity.

- Implicit premise: Structural divides between laboring masses and elite minorities are inevitable under current legal frameworks.
