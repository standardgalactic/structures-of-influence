**Central Thesis**

Peter Hitchens argues in *They Destroyed Family and Morality* that a profound cultural revolution occurring between the 1960s and 1970s in Britain dismantled historically rooted Christian and hierarchical values, replacing them with a ruthless secular and managerial order permeating law, media, crime, education, and social norms. This transformation is portrayed as an irreversible shift in national identity.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Hitchens contends that this cultural revolution mirrors the radical changes of Mao’s Chinese Cultural Revolution in scale and impact. He illustrates how traditional British institutions—such as community policing exemplified by *Dixon of Dock Green*—were supplanted by bureaucratic, reactive approaches like *Z-Cars*. The shift is further symbolized by Tony Blair’s handling of Princess Diana’s death (1997), where dissenting opinions were suppressed under political correctness, signifying the culmination of this cultural transformation.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

1. **Historical Analogy**: Hitchens draws parallels with Mao’s Cultural Revolution to emphasize the radical nature of the British shift.
2. **Managerial State**: The concept is used to describe how post‑war Britain became increasingly dominated by bureaucratic, state‑controlled mechanisms that replaced traditional moral and familial structures.
3. **Political Correctness**: Highlighted as a tool for suppressing dissenting opinions, exemplified by Blair’s response to Diana’s death.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

- **Shift in Law Enforcement**: Transition from community policing (e.g., *Dixon of Dock Green*) to more bureaucratic approaches (e.g., *Z-Cars*), symbolizing the move from preventative to reactive policing.
- **Tony Blair as End Point**: His handling of Princess Diana’s death (1997) is cited as a manifestation of political correctness and managerial state control, illustrating how dissenting views were silenced.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

1. The 1960s‑1970s revolution was significant enough to alter societal structures fundamentally.
2. Changes occurred covertly rather than through overt public debate.
3. Replacement of traditional British values with a secular managerial order represents an irreversible change in national identity.

**Qualifications and Counterarguments**

- **Objection**: Critics may argue that the changes are not as radical or pervasive as Hitchens suggests, pointing to ongoing debates without attributing them solely to this period.
- **Unresolved Questions**: The extent of economic versus social/political factors driving these transformations remains debated. Class dynamics' role in accelerating these changes is also unresolved.
