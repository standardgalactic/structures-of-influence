**Central Thesis**

Thomas Hobbes's argument in *Leviathan* posits that human beings, driven by self‑interest and fear in a state of nature, must surrender much of their freedom to an absolute sovereign—monarchy—to escape perpetual conflict and establish lasting social order.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Hobbes begins with the premise that humans are naturally inclined toward competition and survival over cooperation. In his view, without a governing authority, individuals would engage in a “war of all against all,” where self‑preservation leads to mutual destruction due to inherent selfishness and fear (Chapter 2). To avoid this chaotic condition, society must consent to an absolute sovereign capable of enforcing laws through the threat of punishment. This transition from a state of nature to civil society is framed as a rational social contract wherein individuals relinquish most freedoms in exchange for security and order provided by a sovereign.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**

The argument draws heavily on Hobbes’s concept of the “state of nature,” which he describes as solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. This notion contrasts with later philosophical perspectives, such as John Locke’s *Second Treatise on Government* (1689), where individuals retain natural rights and consent to government for protection rather than surrendering all liberties.

**Evidence and Illustrations**

Hobbes uses the historical context of the English Civil War to illustrate his points. The instability during that period serves as a backdrop, reinforcing his argument that without an absolute monarch, societies risk internal strife and external threats. His analysis is grounded in the belief that only a sovereign with unquestioned authority can deter potential aggressors through fear of punishment.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

The implicit premises include:
1. Human beings inherently act out of self‑interest.
2. The absence of governance leads to mutual destruction due to competition for survival.
3. An absolute monarch can effectively prevent conflict by deterring threats through the threat of punishment rather than positive governance.

Inferential gaps arise in considering how these principles apply across different cultural and historical contexts, where human cooperation and altruism may play significant roles beyond Hobbes’s pessimistic view.

**Qualifications and Counterarguments**

Critics argue that Hobbes’s depiction of human nature is overly deterministic and ignores the capacity for social bonds, empathy, and cooperative behavior. They contend that modern societies demonstrate that individuals can coexist peacefully without resorting to absolute monarchy. Additionally, some scholars question whether an absolute monarch inherently leads to tyranny, suggesting that checks and balances are necessary to prevent abuse of power.

**Relation to the Study of Institutional and Cultural Influence**

Hobbes’s argument highlights the influence of political philosophy on institutional design, emphasizing how historical contexts shape governance structures. His work reflects a cultural perspective prevalent in 17th‑century England, where stability was paramount due to ongoing civil conflicts. Understanding Hobbes within this framework helps analyze how different societies interpret and apply his principles in varying cultural and historical settings.
