Central Thesis:
Christopher Lasch argues that modern society is characterized by a pervasive narcissism rooted in the decline of traditional social structures—particularly the old petty bourgeois order—which once limited aspirations to local community, family, and craft rather than global mobility. This shift creates an environment where status seeking becomes paramount, leading individuals to scramble for higher social standing and personal recognition, thus fostering widespread narcissistic tendencies.

Argument Reconstruction:
Lasch contends that historical context plays a crucial role in understanding this phenomenon. The old petty bourgeoisie placed limits on the “cult of mobility,” emphasizing values such as family, craft, neighborhood solidarity, and a sense that life was merely a waiting room for something better (Lasch, *The Culture of Narcissism*). In contrast, contemporary elites—meritocratic, cosmopolitan, credentialed, and professional—are mobile across national borders, treating the population as a problem to be managed rather than an integral part of their identity or community (Lasch, *The Revolt of the Elites*). This cultural shift results in narcissistic tendencies because it disconnects individuals from rootedness and communal identity.
