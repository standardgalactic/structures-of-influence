**Central Thesis**

Modern therapy is presented by Philip Rieff as a new “religion” that replaces traditional spiritual, familial, and communal authority structures with an internalized analytic gaze. This shift occurs because contemporary individuals are increasingly subject to external therapeutic authority rather than binding moral imperatives traditionally imposed by churches or families. The argument posits that the loss of conviction among elite members—those who should have transmitted cultural norms and values—marks the beginning of a broader cultural decline, where charisma without an accompanying creed becomes unsustainable.

**Argument Reconstruction**

1. **Therapeutic Authority as Replacement**: Rieff argues that in modern societies, the therapeutic paradigm has supplanted older forms of authority (e.g., religious or familial commands). Individuals are now interrogated by external “authority” through psychoanalytic tools, eroding the binding force of traditional moral interdicts.

2. **Cultural Decline Begins with Elites**: The decline is traced to elite members—those who should embody cultural norms—who no longer find internalized commands compelling. This shift reflects a broader societal trend where charismatic authority (traditionally rooted in spiritual or familial frameworks) gives way to therapeutic authority, which lacks an inherent creed.

3. **Charisma Without Creed Is Unsustainable**: Rieff asserts that charisma inherently requires an accompanying creed; without it, the transformative power of such leadership over individuals and society dissipates. Historical examples from various cultures illustrate this pattern: Moscow’s decline under Parto, Arnold Toyby’s theories on elite theory, and Oswald Spangler’s analysis of charismatic authority.

**Implicit Premises and Inferential Gaps**

- **Counterargument on Therapeutic Functionality**: Critics might argue that therapy can also serve as a means for personal empowerment rather than merely replacing religious authority, suggesting a more nuanced role.
