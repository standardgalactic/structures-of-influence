**Central Thesis**

Gaetano Mosca’s theory posits that every society is governed by an organized minority—what he terms the “ruling class” or “organized minority”—which dominates over a larger disorganized majority. This structural reality arises from inherent differences in power, organization, and capacity for coordination between these two groups. The ruling minority maintains its dominance through both coercive force (when necessary) and consent (through ideological mechanisms), ensuring that political power remains concentrated among those who can effectively organize and manage the masses.

**Argument Reconstruction**

Mosca’s argument is built on several interrelated premises:

1. **Existence of an Organized Minority**: In any given society, a small group with distinct interests inevitably emerges as the ruling class. This minority possesses superior organization, discipline, and coordination capabilities that allow it to outmaneuver larger, less structured groups.

2. **Contrast with Disorganized Masses**: The disorganized majority lacks these structural advantages, leading to its continual subordination by the organized minority. Historical examples—such as trained soldiers defeating untrained masses—illustrate this principle of superior organization translating into political and military dominance.

3. **Structural Determinants of Power**: Mosca argues that the distribution of power is not a matter of popular consent but rather an outcome of inherent class differences. The ruling minority’s ability to impose its will stems from its capacity for collective action, which the disorganized masses cannot replicate.

4. **Implications for Governance**: Because political power inherently separates into distinct classes with differing interests and capacities for organization, governance systems must accommodate this reality. This leads to a system where elite rule is both inevitable and necessary for effective administration.
