**Central Thesis**

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) successfully argued against Morton Salt Company’s violation of the Robinson‑Patman Act (1936), asserting that the law was intended to prevent large retailers from gaining an unfair competitive advantage through discriminatory pricing practices. This ruling underscores a broader legal and economic debate over whether price discrimination based on volume purchasing constitutes anti‑competitive behavior, particularly when smaller retailers are disadvantaged.

**Argument Reconstruction**

The lecture outlines several key arguments:

1. **Legal Framework**: The Robinson‑Patman Act was enacted to prohibit “price discrimination” that would give chain stores an unfair advantage over independent retailers by allowing them to purchase goods at lower prices than their competitors. This law reflects a concern for maintaining competitive equity among retail businesses of different scales.

2. **Morton Salt’s Alleged Violation**: Morton Salt was accused of violating the Act when it offered its “blue label” salt at a purportedly discounted price exclusively to five national chain stores that purchased sufficient quantities to qualify for this discount. The speaker emphasizes that these discounts were not accessible to smaller retailers, thereby creating an inequitable market environment.

3. **Supreme Court Ruling**: The Supreme Court ruled in favor of the FTC, interpreting the legislative history of the Robinson‑Patman Act as indicating Congress’s intent to prevent large buyers from securing a competitive edge solely because they could purchase larger quantities at lower unit prices. This interpretation aligns with the original purpose of the act—protecting smaller retailers from being priced out by larger competitors.

**Intellectual Sources and Concepts**
