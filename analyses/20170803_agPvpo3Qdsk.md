**Central Thesis**

The lecture argues that the transition from feudalism to capitalism in England during the 16th century was driven primarily by demographic growth, urbanization, and a shift toward commercial economic activities—especially textile production—which transformed societal values from land‑based rents to profit‑driven meritocracy. This transformation is framed as occurring despite lingering religious and cultural critiques of commerce.

**Argument Reconstruction**

1. **Demographic Growth and Urbanization**: The Black Death initially reduced England’s population, but by the mid-16th century, London experienced rapid urban expansion (from ~3 million to ~4.5 million inhabitants by 1616). This growth signaled a move from agrarian feudalism toward an urban, capitalist economy.

2. **Migration and Economic Roles**: Peasants migrated into urban labor roles as merchants, artisans, and actors, indicating the emergence of a new class structure based on trade rather than inherited land ownership.

3. **Economic Dependence on Textiles**: By this period, England’s economy was heavily reliant on woolen cloth exports, accounting for roughly one-third of all exports. This export focus highlighted the importance of commercial activity in driving national wealth and international influence.

4. **Transition to Capitalism**: The shift from feudal structures—where power derived from land ownership—to a capitalist system where wealth accumulation through profit became central was driven by both economic necessity (e.g., textile demand) and broader cultural changes (e.g., Protestant work ethic).
